# What a deep dive is (and why most people skip the work)

<div class="learn" markdown="1">
**What you will learn**

- What a deep dive actually is, in one sentence
- Why the company's own filings beat any summary website
- Why most people never do one, and why that is your advantage
- How this module will build a full deep dive on Apple, step by step
</div>

Imagine buying a used phone from a stranger. You can take the seller's word that it works perfectly, or you can switch it on, check the battery health, look for cracks and test the camera yourself. Checking takes twenty minutes and a bit of knowledge. Most people skip it, and then act surprised when the battery dies in a month.

A deep dive is the checking. It means reading a company's own reports, building the important numbers yourself, and only then deciding what you think the business is worth. Not a summary of the reports. Not a video about the reports. The reports themselves.

In America, every listed company must file these reports with the Securities and Exchange Commission, or SEC, which is the market regulator, roughly the American cousin of SEBI in India. The big one is called the 10-K: an annual report that the law forces the company to write, covering the business, the risks, and three years of audited accounts. Apple's 10-K for fiscal 2025 was filed on 31 October 2025, and it is free for anyone on Earth to read on SEC.gov's system, called EDGAR. Between 10-Ks come the 10-Qs, shorter quarterly updates, and once a year the proxy statement, which tells you who owns the company and what the bosses are paid.

![The table of contents of Apple's 10-K on SEC EDGAR]({{ "/assets/shots/edgar-10k-toc.png" | relative_url }})

Notice the shape of the document: Item 1 describes the business, Item 1A lists the risk factors, and Item 8 holds the financial statements. Every US company follows this same skeleton, so once you learn to read one 10-K, you can read any of them.

Here is what a screen gives you for free, and it is genuinely useful:

![The summary statistics panel for Apple on StockAnalysis.com]({{ "/assets/shots/sa-aapl-overview.png" | relative_url }})

Notice how much is there: price, market value, ratios, all computed for you. And notice what is missing: where the numbers came from, what was left out, and whether the year was unusual. A screen can tell you Apple's revenue was $416.2 billion in fiscal 2025 (about Rs 36 lakh crore, at roughly 87 rupees to a dollar). It cannot tell you that the year before carried a one time tax charge of $10.2 billion that made growth look worse than it was. That sentence is in the 10-K, in a note on income taxes, and we will open it in chapter 6.

So why does almost nobody do this? Three honest reasons. It is slow: a proper first pass over a company takes a weekend, not ten minutes. Filings look intimidating, all tables and legal words, until you learn that you only need five or six sections. And screens feel like knowledge. A ratio with two decimal places looks precise even when the inputs were stitched together carelessly. In the Indian deep dive that this module's Apple dive mirrors, the exported data disagreed with the audited accounts on eleven figures, including the one the whole analysis leaned on hardest. The person who reads the filing catches that. The person who reads the screen never knows.

That is why the skipped work is good news for you. If most investors will not read the document, then reading it is an edge that costs nothing but time. A twelve year old with a laptop and a browser can see exactly what a fund manager in New York sees. The filings are the level playing field, and almost nobody stands on it.

One last thing before we start, because it trips up every beginner: Apple's financial year is not January to December. Its fiscal year is the 52 or 53 week period ending on the last Saturday of September (the 10-K says exactly that). So fiscal 2025 ended on 27 September 2025. When this module says a year, it means Apple's year.

<div class="try" markdown="1">
**Try it yourself**

Go to sec.gov and open EDGAR's company search. Type "Apple" and find the company page (its CIK, its SEC identity number, is 0000320193). Open the most recent 10-K and search inside it for "total net sales". Check that the figure for 2025 is $416,161 million. You have just read your first line of a real filing. Ten minutes, free, no account needed.
</div>

<div class="takeaways" markdown="1">
**Key takeaways**

- A deep dive means reading the company's own filings and building the numbers yourself, before forming a view.
- The 10-K is the annual source of record; 10-Qs update it quarterly; the proxy covers ownership and pay.
- Summary screens are a starting point, not an answer. They cannot tell you what was unusual about the year.
- The work is slow, and that is exactly why it still works.
- Apple's fiscal year ends on the last Saturday of September, not in December.
</div>

**Read one real thing**: [Apple's fiscal 2025 Form 10-K on SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/320193/000032019325000079/aapl-20250927.htm). Notice Item 1A, Risk Factors: the company must list, in plain English, everything that could hurt the business. Most investors never read a single one.
