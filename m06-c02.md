# What an RRG is: the four quadrants in plain words

<div class="learn">
**What you will learn**

- What an RRG chart is and who made it
- What the horizontal and vertical axes measure
- The four quadrants and what each one says
- Why the centre of the chart is the benchmark itself
</div>

In the last chapter you learned that relative strength is the gap between a stock and the market. Now imagine plotting that gap for many stocks or sectors on one map, so you can see the whole race at a glance. That map exists. It is called a **Relative Rotation Graph**, usually shortened to **RRG**, and it was invented by a Dutch analyst named Julius de Kempenaer. You can view it free on StockCharts.com.

## The two axes: level and speed

A cricket analogy helps. Think of a batsman's form. Two questions matter. First: is he scoring more than the team average this season? That is his level. Second: is his scoring getting better or worse lately? That is his direction. A batsman can be above average but declining, or below average but improving fast.

An RRG plots every stock or sector using those same two questions:

- **Horizontal axis (left to right): RS-Ratio.** This measures the level of relative strength. To the right of centre, the stock is beating the benchmark. To the left, it is losing to the benchmark.
- **Vertical axis (up and down): RS-Momentum.** This measures whether that relative strength is speeding up or slowing down. Above centre, momentum is improving. Below centre, it is fading.

Both numbers are built from the relative strength idea of chapter 1, just smoothed and standardised so everything sits on one scale. Around 100 is the centre, which represents the benchmark itself. The benchmark always sits in the middle of its own map, the way the average runner defines the middle of the race.

## The four quadrants

The two axes cut the map into four boxes, called **quadrants** (quadrant just means one of four parts). Each quadrant has a name and a colour on StockCharts:

```
                 RS-Momentum (up = improving)
                          |
        IMPROVING         |         LEADING
   (below the market,     |   (beating the market,
    but getting stronger) |    and getting stronger)
                          |
   -----------------------+-----------------------  RS-Ratio (right = beating)
                          |
        LAGGING           |         WEAKENING
   (below the market,     |   (beating the market,
    and getting weaker)   |    but losing steam)
                          |
```

In plain words:

- **Leading** (top right): beating the benchmark, and the lead is growing. The star batsman in form.
- **Weakening** (top right, lower half): still beating the benchmark, but the lead is shrinking. The star is getting tired.
- **Lagging** (bottom left): losing to the benchmark, and getting worse. Out of form and out of luck.
- **Improving** (top left): still losing to the benchmark, but catching up. The underdog finding form.

## Why "rotation"?

Watch the map over several weeks and something lovely appears: stocks and sectors tend to travel **clockwise** through the quadrants. Improving turns into leading, leading cools into weakening, weakening sinks into lagging, lagging recovers into improving. That circular journey is why it is called a rotation graph. Chapter 3 is all about reading those journeys.

**Try it yourself**

Open the free RRG page on StockCharts.com (search "StockCharts RRG" and choose the Relative Rotation Graphs tool). Set it to the S&P sector view. Write down which sectors sit in Leading today, and which sit in Lagging. Do not judge whether that is good or bad yet. Just practise naming quadrants.
</div>

<div class="takeaways">
**Key takeaways**

- An RRG plots relative strength level (RS-Ratio, sideways) against its momentum (RS-Momentum, up and down).
- The centre of the chart is the benchmark; everything else is measured against it.
- Four quadrants: Leading, Weakening, Lagging, Improving.
- Leading does not mean "buy" and Lagging does not mean "sell". They describe position in the race, nothing more.
</div>
