# Industry and moat: the castle and the moat around it

<div class="learn" markdown="1">
**What you will learn**

- What a moat is, and the two questions to ask about any of them
- Where moats show up in the accounts, so you do not have to take anyone's word
- Apple's moat, measured from its own filings
- Why the strongest part of Apple's moat is also the part under attack
</div>

Two kirana stores sell the same biscuits at the same price on the same street. One has a queue every evening because it is inside the metro station exit; the other watches people walk past. The first store has a moat: something that stops customers from drifting to a competitor, even when the competitor sells the identical thing.

Investors call any durable advantage like that a moat, after the water around a castle. When you study one, ask two separate questions. How **deep** is it: how much more does the business earn than an ordinary competitor? And how **wide** is it: how many years can it keep doing that before the water drains? A shop can have one great year because a festival fell right. That is depth for a moment, not width.

Here is the part beginners miss: moats are visible in the numbers, if you know where to look. The first place to check is gross margin, the share of each rupee of sales left after making the product. A company with no moat competes on price, and competing on price squeezes gross margin. A company with a deep moat charges more than it costs, year after year.

![Apple's ratio history on StockAnalysis.com]({{ "/assets/shots/sa-aapl-ratios.png" | relative_url }})

Notice the gross margin line in Apple's own filings: 39.1% in fiscal 2016, then 41.8%, 43.3%, 44.1%, 46.2% and 46.9% across fiscal 2021 to 2025. Five rises in a row, in an industry where the 10-K itself warns of "aggressive price competition" and "downward pressure on gross margins". Everyone else's margin gets squeezed; Apple's keeps climbing. That is depth, measured, not asserted.

What is the moat made of? Three things, and you can test each in the accounts. The **ecosystem**: your photos, messages, health data, paired watch and paid apps all live inside Apple's world, so leaving feels like moving houses. The **brand**, which lets a phone costing under $600 to make sell for far more (an iPhone at $1,000 is about Rs 87,000, and people queue for it). And the **services toll**: once 26.2% of revenue comes from services at a 75.4% gross margin, each device sold is a shopfront rented out forever.

Now the honest half, because a moat story without an attack plan is a fairy tale. The services toll is the most profitable water in Apple's moat, and it is exactly the part under legal attack: courts in America and regulators in Europe are forcing changes to how the App Store can charge. And width has a second enemy: the industry itself. The phone market is a replacement market now, meaning almost everyone who wants one already has one and buys only to upgrade. iPhone revenue was $192.0 billion in fiscal 2021 and $209.6 billion in fiscal 2025: about 2.2% a year. The castle is strong; the land around it has stopped growing. Both halves belong in your notes.

![Microsoft's summary panel on StockAnalysis.com]({{ "/assets/shots/sa-msft-overview.png" | relative_url }})

Notice Microsoft's margins beside Apple's: a 67.9% gross margin against Apple's 46.9%, because selling software copies costs almost nothing. Comparing across an industry teaches you what "high" means; comparing within one teaches you who holds the moat. A drinks company like Coca-Cola makes a third kind of comparison: brand moat, no ecosystem at all.

One warning before you go hunting moats everywhere: a high margin in a single year proves nothing. It is the direction across five to ten years that separates a moat from a lucky season. That is why the next chapter builds ten years in one table before allowing any conclusions.

<div class="try" markdown="1">
**Try it yourself**

Open the ratios or financials pages for [Apple](https://stockanalysis.com/stocks/AAPL/financials/ratios/?ref=MICROCAPMINUTE), [Microsoft](https://stockanalysis.com/stocks/MSFT/?ref=MICROCAPMINUTE) and [Coca-Cola](https://stockanalysis.com/stocks/KO/?ref=MICROCAPMINUTE). Write down each company's gross margin for the latest year and for ten years ago. Which improved, which held, which fell? You have just ranked three moats by depth, using one number and ten minutes.
</div>

<div class="takeaways" markdown="1">
**Key takeaways**

- A moat is a durable advantage that keeps customers from leaving. Ask how deep it is and how wide it is, separately.
- Moats show up in gross margin: direction over five to ten years, never one year alone.
- Apple's moat is its ecosystem, its brand and the services toll, and the margin history proves the first two daily.
- The most profitable part of the moat, the App Store toll, is the part regulators are attacking. Write the attack down beside the moat, always.
</div>

**Read one real thing**: [the Competition paragraph in Item 1 of Apple's 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/320193/000032019325000079/aapl-20250927.htm). Notice that the company admits, in its own legal document, that its markets have "aggressive price competition" and "price sensitivity". When a company confesses a weakness in a filing, believe it, and then check whether the margins agree.
