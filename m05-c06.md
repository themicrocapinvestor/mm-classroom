# Audit your own work: the pre-mortem checklist

<div class="learn" markdown="1">
**What you will learn**

- What a pre-mortem is, and why you run one on your own analysis
- The six checks we ran on the Apple deep dive, with real catches
- How to mark figures you cannot verify instead of hiding them
- Why the notes to the accounts beat every screen ever made
</div>

You know the last ten minutes of an exam, when you check your own paper? Nobody enjoys it. You have already written the answers, and part of you does not want to know. But that is where the marks are rescued. A deep dive needs the same ritual, and it has a name: a pre-mortem. Before anyone else reads your work, assume it is wrong, and go find where.

Here is the checklist we ran on the Apple dive, and what each check actually caught.

**1. Trace every figure.** Each number gets a source: a filing, or a named page. No exceptions, no "everyone knows". If a figure cannot be traced, it either leaves the document or stays with a visible flag.

**2. Cross check wherever two sources cover the same number.** Apple's cash and marketable securities in June 2026: the market data page said $146.52 billion; adding the three lines from the filing itself gave $146.5 billion. Debt: $84.34 billion against $84.3 billion. Employees: 166,000 in both. When independent routes agree, you can relax. When they do not, the filing wins, always.

**3. Mark your own arithmetic.** Some figures are computed, not reported. Apple never publishes a September quarter table, so our fourth quarter is the full year minus the nine months: computed, and labelled as such in the table. The early EPS figures are adjusted for the 2020 four for one stock split: computed, and labelled. A computed figure is not a sin; an unlabelled one is.

**4. Flag what you cannot verify, out loud.** Two figures in the dive carry flags. The roughly $20 billion a year that Google reportedly pays Apple to stay the default search engine appears in no Apple filing; it surfaced in a court case (that is about Rs 1.7 lakh crore a year, if true, so it matters). And the "2.35 billion active devices" claim came from a company earnings call, not a filing. Both stayed in the document, because both are important, and both say "not verified" beside them. An honest flag makes a document more trustworthy, not less.

**5. Record disagreements instead of smoothing them.** The market value on the statistics page, $4.57 trillion, implies a slightly higher share count than the 14.594 billion on the 10-Q cover. The difference is under 2% and probably reflects how share based pay is counted, and it is written down in the audit file rather than silently averaged away. Small unexplained gaps are where big mistakes hide.

**6. Hunt the one offs.** Fiscal 2024 profit fell, which looks like decline until Note 7 of the 10-K explains it: a one time tax charge of $10.2 billion after a European court confirmed an Irish tax ruling. Without it, profit rose. And here is the part worth sitting with: the dispute began in 2016, and the money sat in escrow, disclosed in the notes, for eight years before the charge landed. Anyone reading the contingency notes knew it was there. Every screen missed it, because screens do not read notes.

![The risk factors section of Apple's 10-K on SEC EDGAR]({{ "/assets/shots/edgar-10k-risk.png" | relative_url }})

Notice how much a company must confess: tariffs, single source suppliers, App Store regulation. The pre-mortem reads these first, because they are the company's own list of what could break your thesis.

![EDGAR's full text search with a query typed in]({{ "/assets/shots/edgar-fts.png" | relative_url }})

Notice the search box: every filing of every US company, searchable. Type "State Aid" or "tariff" and you are reading what the professionals read, at the same speed, for free.

That is the whole ritual: trace, cross check, label your arithmetic, flag the unverifiable, record disagreements, hunt one offs. It takes an hour, it is boring, and it is the hour that separates analysis from content. The market will run its own audit on your work eventually, and it charges tuition. Better to fail your own exam first.

<div class="try" markdown="1">
**Try it yourself**

Pick one number from any summary page, say Apple's 166,000 employees, and find it in the [fiscal 2025 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/320193/000032019325000079/aapl-20250927.htm) (search "full-time equivalent"). Then find one number on any website that you cannot locate in any filing, and write "not verified" beside it in your notebook. Congratulations: you have done your first audit, and it only took ten minutes.
</div>

<div class="takeaways" markdown="1">
**Key takeaways**

- A pre-mortem: assume your analysis is wrong, and find where before the market does.
- Trace every figure; where two sources cover one number, they must agree, and the filing wins.
- Label computed figures; flag unverifiable ones; never smooth over a disagreement.
- The notes to the accounts (contingencies, tax notes) hold the stories screens cannot show.
</div>

**Read one real thing**: [Note 7, Income Taxes, in Apple's 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/320193/000032019325000079/aapl-20250927.htm): search the page for "State Aid". Notice the tone: no drama, just dates, courts and amounts, ending with a $10.2 billion charge. This is what a risk looks like eight years before it becomes news.
