# Valuation: a DCF simple enough to argue with

<div class="learn">
**What you will learn**

- What a discounted cash flow is, using a mango tree
- The five ingredients, and where each one honestly comes from
- Our Apple DCF, with the answer and the full sensitivity grid
- The reverse DCF: asking what the price already believes
</div>

A farmer offers to sell you a mango tree. What is it worth? Not the wood. It is worth the mangoes: the fruit it will give over its life. But a basket of mangoes in 2035 is worth less to you than the same basket today, because today's basket could be sold and the money put to work immediately. So you count the future baskets, shrink each one a little for every year you must wait, and add them up. That shrinking is called discounting, and the whole exercise is a discounted cash flow, or DCF.

Companies are mango trees that drop cash instead of fruit. The recipe has five ingredients:

1. **A forecast of free cash flow** for the next few years. Free cash flow is the cash left after running and maintaining the business.
2. **A terminal value**: one number standing in for all the years after the forecast, growing slowly forever.
3. **A discount rate**, called WACC: the yearly shrink, the return you demand for waiting and for risk.
4. **Net cash**: cash and investments minus borrowings, added at the end because it belongs to shareholders over and above the business.
5. **The share count**, to turn a company value into a per share value.

Every ingredient of ours has a name and address, because a DCF you cannot argue with is decoration. The forecast starts from the analyst consensus on [Apple's forecast page](https://stockanalysis.com/stocks/AAPL/forecast/?ref=MICROCAPMINUTE): revenue of $477.4 billion for fiscal 2026 and $523.3 billion for 2027, then our own slowing assumptions of 7%, 6% and 5%. Operating margin is held at 32.6%, the level the consensus implies, and after 17% tax, that operating profit is the free cash flow, because Apple's depreciation and its capital spending are both about 2.5% of sales and cancel out. Net cash is $62.2 billion from the June 2026 balance sheet. The share count is 14.594 billion from the 10-Q cover page.

![Apple's analyst forecast page on StockAnalysis.com]({{ "/assets/shots/sa-aapl-forecast.png" | relative_url }})

Notice that the consensus is not magic: it is 31 people's estimates averaged. Use it as a starting point to argue with, not as an answer.

The discount rate deserves its own paragraph, because it is where DCFs are quietly rigged. We build it from parts: the US ten year government bond paid 4.56% on 10 August 2026 (the closest thing to a risk free return), plus a premium for owning stocks of 4.23% (a public estimate kept by a New York University finance professor), times Apple's beta of 1.09, which measures how much the stock swings versus the market. That is 4.56 + 1.09 × 4.23, about 9.2%. Terminal growth is set at 3.0%, below the bond rate, on purpose: nothing grows faster than the economy forever.

The answer: **$168 a share**. The price on 10 August 2026 was $308.26. So we run the grid, changing the discount rate and terminal growth together:

| Discount rate | Terminal 2.5% | Terminal 3.0% | Terminal 3.5% |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8.50% | $175.84 | $188.49 | $203.66 |
| 9.17% | $158.33 | $168.23 | $179.86 |
| 10.00% | $140.99 | $148.51 | $157.19 |

Nine cells, and every one sits below the price. Read a grid by its shape, not its favourite cell: the kindest reasonable assumptions still produce $204.

Then the move that separates honest valuation from salesmanship: run it backwards. Forget what Apple is worth and ask what $308.26 already believes. Answer: about $273.8 billion of free cash flow every year, growing 3% forever, starting now. That is double the $136.7 billion Apple actually produced in the last twelve months. Equivalently, the price assumes roughly 6.2% growth forever, above the risk free rate, in perpetuity. You may still decide to own it after knowing that (many do, for many reasons), but you will know you are paying for a belief, not for the cash.

Now the plain talk the style of this classroom requires. A DCF often fails, and here is exactly how: at our central inputs, 76% of the answer is the terminal value, the part after 2030 that nobody can see. A five year DCF is mostly an opinion about year six onwards wearing arithmetic as a disguise. So treat it as an argument with its assumptions printed beside it. The moment someone shows you a DCF without the assumptions, close it.

![Apple's dividend page on StockAnalysis.com]({{ "/assets/shots/sa-aapl-dividend.png" | relative_url }})

Notice the dividend yield, about 0.35%, and the buyback yield, about 2.13%. Together that is the cash a holder actually receives each year, roughly 2.5%. Everything above that must come from growth. When the grid says $168 to $204 and the price says $308, the gap is the growth the buyer is trusting.

<div class="try">
**Try it yourself**

Open Apple's forecast page and find the consensus revenue for this fiscal year and next. Compute the growth rate yourself (next divided by this, minus one). Now ask the only question that matters in any DCF: if the year after grows at 3% instead of 7%, does the thesis still work? Write one sentence answering it. You have just stress tested a valuation, which is the whole skill.
</div>

<div class="takeaways">
**Key takeaways**

- A DCF adds up future cash, shrunk for waiting. Five ingredients: forecast, terminal value, discount rate, net cash, shares.
- Build the discount rate from visible parts: 4.56% risk free, plus 1.09 beta times a 4.23% stock premium, giving about 9.2%.
- Our Apple answer is $168, the grid runs $141 to $204, and the price was $308. Every defensible cell sat below the price.
- The reverse DCF is the honest question: the price assumes double today's free cash flow, forever. A DCF is an argument, never a prophecy.
</div>

**Read one real thing**: [the US Treasury's daily yield curve page](https://home.treasury.gov/resource-center/data-chart-center/interest-rates/TextView?type=daily_treasury_yield_curve&field_tdr_date_value_month=202608). Notice the 10 year column: that number is the foundation under every valuation on Earth, including yours, and it changes a little every day.
